AirZone 26

Full mastering suite for REAPER — VST3 & CLAP · Windows 10/11 · macOS Apple Silicon

Version 0.2.0 · by crowelian 2026

1. Introduction

AirZone 26 is the last plug-in on your song. You put it on REAPER's master track and it makes the finished mix sound more polished and louder, without it getting distorted. Think of it as the finishing touch before you publish a track.

AirZone 26 is a mastering plug-in written in Rust. It puts twenty-three processing modules, a metering section, a Master Assistant and 82 factory presets behind a single plug-in window, so you can take a finished stereo mix to a release-ready master on the master track of one REAPER project.

Honest scope note. AirZone 26 is an independent project inspired by the workflow of modern all-in-one mastering suites. All algorithms are original. In particular, Stem EQ is a signal-processing approximation (harmonic/percussive and stereo-centre analysis) and not a neural-network separator, and the Unlimiter is an upward expander, not a trained declipper. Both are excellent for corrections of a few dB; they will not perform miracles on extreme material.

2. Installation

Copy the plug-in files into the folder your DAW scans, then tell REAPER to re-scan. If you do not see it in the FX list afterwards, the files are in the wrong folder or REAPER has not re-scanned yet.

Download the build for your system from the project's Actions → Build artifacts or Releases page. Each ZIP contains two plug-ins in VST3 and CLAP format - AirZone 26 (the mastering suite) and AirZone 26 Tonal Balance (a companion meter, see below) - and this manual.

REAPER setup

  1. VST3: Options → Preferences → Plug-ins → VST, make sure the VST3 folder above is listed, then click Re-scan (or Clear cache and re-scan).
  2. CLAP: REAPER 7 and newer load CLAP natively from the CLAP folders; use Options → Preferences → Plug-ins → CLAP if you keep them elsewhere.
  3. Insert it: on the master track click FX → Add → search AirZone 26.
  4. Put AirZone 26 last in the master FX chain (after any metering you want to keep post-master). Tick Process stereo only is not needed; the plug-in is stereo in / stereo out.
Tip: right-click the plug-in's FX window title → Pin and use Float for a larger window; the editor is resizable by your host's window frame.

3. Quick start

Fastest way to a result: load a preset, play the loudest part of your song, and listen. If it sounds better and not squashed, you are done. Use Bypass to compare with the original.
  1. Put the plug-in on the master track and play your song.
  2. Pick a starting preset from the preset menu at the top (for example Universal Master), or click 🎛 Master Assistant and let it listen to the loudest part of the song.
  3. Play the loudest chorus. Watch the Integrated and True pk meters at the bottom. The Maximizer Gain control sets how hard you push into the ceiling.
  4. Click modules in the left rail to edit them. The check box next to a name turns it on or off.
  5. Toggle Bypass (top bar) to compare with the original. Bypass is latency-compensated so the comparison stays aligned, but it is not loudness matched — adjust for level when judging.
  6. Save your result with 💾 Save.

5. Signal chain

Audio flows through the modules from top to bottom, like water through pipes. The order matters: fix problems first, add colour next, make it loud last.

Audio enters through Input gain, passes through every enabled module in the order shown in the rail, goes through Output gain and the optional Dither, and leaves. A disabled module costs nothing and adds no latency.

Default order:

The FFT group

Stem EQ, Match EQ, Stabilizer and Spectral Shaper (and Pitch Correction when set to Full Song mode) work in the frequency domain (2048-point STFT, 75 % overlap). They share a single analysis/synthesis pass so enabling all four costs the same latency as enabling one (2048 samples). The shared pass runs at the position of the first enabled FFT module in the chain, and the enabled modules inside it always run in the order Match EQ → Stem EQ → Stabilizer → Spectral Shaper → Full-Song tuning.

Order guidelines

  • Corrective first (Unlimiter, EQ, Dynamic EQ, Stabilizer) then dynamics, then colour, then width, then limiting last.
  • Keep the Maximizer at the end — it guarantees the ceiling, so nothing after it should add level.
  • Put the Imager after saturation: harmonics generated by the Exciter can then be widened too.
  • Use Reset chain order in the rail to return to the default.

6. Modules

A module is one tool, like an equalizer or a compressor. Every module starts off, so nothing changes until you tick its box. Each has its own preset menu: pick one, then tweak.

Every parameter is also listed with its range and default in the parameter reference.

Equalizer

Turns bass, mids and treble up or down. Too boomy? Pull the low band down. Too dull? Lift the top.

Eight bands. Each band has an on switch, a Type (Bell, Low Shelf, High Shelf, High Pass, Low Pass, Notch), Frequency, Gain, Q, Slope (12/24/36/48 dB/oct for the pass filters) and a Placement:

  • Stereo – both channels. Mid / Side – only the mid or side signal (for example a side-only high shelf for air). Left / Right – one channel.

The curve shows the combined response. Band defaults are laid out as a typical mastering EQ: 1 high-pass, 2 low shelf, 3–6 bells, 7 high shelf, 8 low-pass.

Dynamic EQ

An equalizer that only acts when a frequency gets too loud, for example taming a harsh sound only when it is loud.

Four bell filters whose gain follows the level in their own band. Static Gain is always applied. Dynamic Range is how far the gain may move: negative values cut when the band exceeds Threshold (de-essing, de-muddying), positive values boost. Ratio, Attack and Release shape the response. Use it for problems that come and go.

Match EQ

Listens to a song you like and nudges your tone toward it. It is a starting point: always check by ear.

Captures the average tonal balance (64 log-spaced bands) of two sources and applies the difference.

  1. Play your mix and click ● Learn Source for 20–60 seconds of representative material, then stop.
  2. Play a reference track and click ● Learn Reference (live); or type the path of a WAV file and click Analyze file. (WAV 16/24/32-bit and 32-bit float are supported.)
  3. Enable the module. The curve shows the correction. Amount scales it, Smoothing widens its resolution, and Max Correction limits how far any band may be moved.

Only tonal shape is matched (overall level differences are removed), so loudness is untouched. Captured profiles are saved inside your REAPER project. The plug-in must be playing in real time to learn live; the WAV analysis works offline.

Stabilizer

Smooths out a tonal balance that jumps around, so the mix feels even from start to end.

A slow, adaptive equaliser (32 bands) that nudges the spectrum toward a target slope. It is meant to keep the tonal balance consistent from section to section, not to paint a new sound. Amount scales the effect, Range limits the maximum boost/cut per band, Target Slope is the desired tilt in dB/octave (−4.5 is typical for finished music), Speed sets how fast it adapts, and Use Reference makes it follow the Match EQ reference's shape. The bar chart shows the correction in real time.

Spectral Shaper

Finds frequencies that stick out and squeezes only those, like a very precise "harshness remover".

A frequency-domain resonance suppressor. It compares each FFT bin with the local average (Selectivity sets how many bins around it count as "local") and reduces peaks that stick out by more than Sensitivity dB, by an amount set by Depth, only between Low Limit and High Limit. Tilt adds a gentle spectral tilt. Listen to Delta plays back only what is being removed — use it to be sure you are taking away harshness and not body.

Vintage EQ

An EQ that adds a warm, classic hardware flavour. Gentle moves give a pleasant "analog" tone.

A passive-EQ-style tone shaper. The low section has a Low Frequency selector (20/30/60/100 Hz), Low Boost and Low Atten; using both at once gives the classic "bigger yet tighter" bass. The high section has a High Boost bell with selectable frequency (3–16 kHz) and Bandwidth, and a High Atten shelf with selectable frequency (5/10/20 kHz). Tube adds gentle valve-style output saturation. Knob values 0–10 are not dB; 10 is roughly +14 dB on the low boost.

Dynamics

Makes loud parts quieter so the whole song feels more solid and consistent. Three bands means bass, mids and highs are handled separately.

Three-band compressor with phase-aligned Linkwitz-Riley 4th-order crossovers (Crossover 1/2). Each band has Threshold, Ratio, Knee, Attack, Release and make-up Gain, plus a downward expander (Expander Threshold/Ratio) to lower noise floors. Stereo Link 100 % compresses both channels equally (stable image); lower values react to each channel separately. Mid/Side processes mid and side independently. Gain-reduction meters show each band.

Vintage Comp

A compressor with a vintage character. Higher "ratio" or lower "threshold" means more squeezing; it also adds warmth.

An opto-style compressor: gentle, programme-dependent release made of a fast and a slow stage, so short peaks recover quickly while sustained compression releases slowly. Input drives the detector, Warmth adds valve-style saturation, Mix gives parallel compression.

Unlimiter

If a mix was crushed too hard, this brings some life and punch back by restoring peaks.

Brings back transients on over-limited material by boosting peaks that stand out above the short-term level. Amount scales the effect, Max Boost is the largest boost applied to a peak, Threshold is how far a peak must stand above the body, and Release how fast the boost relaxes. Compensate gently lowers the body to keep loudness similar. Place it first and follow it with the Maximizer. Because the source's lost peaks cannot be recreated exactly, expect an improvement in punch and dynamics rather than a perfect reconstruction.

Impact

Makes drums hit harder or softer by shaping the start of each sound (the "attack").

Three-band attack/sustain shaper. Positive Attack adds snap to drums; negative softens them. Sustain raises or lowers the tail between hits. Use small values (±1–3 dB) on a full mix.

Low End Focus

Tightens the bass so the kick and bass guitar sit together instead of fighting each other.

Works on everything below Focus Frequency: Punch accentuates kicks and bass attacks, Sustain trims or extends their tails, Contrast tightens the low end by adding level-dependent compression.

Bass Control

Keeps the deepest bass steady and, if you like, in the centre (mono) so it plays well on every system.

Splits the low end into Sub (below the Sub/Bass split), Bass (to the Bass/Low-Mid split) and the rest, and gives you separate Gain, Mono (fold to centre) and — for the bass — Punch and Tighten. The three level bars show where your energy sits; Auto Balance slowly adjusts the sub level to reach the Sub-Bass Target (the dB difference between sub and bass). A sub region folded to mono (100 %) is the single easiest way to improve translation to club systems and vinyl.

Clarity

Adds a bit of sparkle and detail to dull-sounding mixes. A little goes a long way.

Brings out quiet detail and air in the upper mids and highs without making loud passages harsh. The band above Lower Edge is watched by a stereo-linked level detector; quiet material in that band is lifted by up to 8 dB (Detail), loud material is left alone. Harmonics adds a little anti-aliased saturation for sparkle, Warmth is a ±3 dB low shelf at 200 Hz to balance a brighter result, and Mix blends the effect in parallel. The Boost bar shows the detail lift being applied. Typical use: Detail 30-50 %, Lower Edge 2 kHz, Harmonics 15-25 %.

Exciter

Adds subtle harmonic "colour" (tape, tube, warmth) so the mix sounds richer and brighter without just boosting treble.

Four-band harmonic exciter with anti-aliased (first-order ADAA) saturation. Mode: Tape (odd harmonics, smooth), Tube and Warm (even harmonics), Retro (brighter, edgier). Each band has Drive (how hard the band is pushed into the shaper), Amount (blend of saturated signal) and Gain. A little Amount on the top band is a classic "air" tool; keep low bands subtle.

Vintage Tape

Imitates a tape machine: soft saturation, gentle glue, slightly rounded highs.

Tape machine emulation: Drive into saturation, selectable Speed (7.5, 15, 30 ips; lower speeds mean a bigger head bump and darker top), Saturation, Wow & Flutter (pitch modulation), Hiss and Age (progressive high-frequency loss). Adds a fixed 16-sample latency.

Imager

Controls how wide the stereo picture is. More width = bigger feeling; too much = hollow in mono. Check the vectorscope.

Four-band stereo width (0 % = mono, 100 % = unchanged, 200 % = double). Stereoize adds a mono-compatible decorrelated component to widen narrow sources. Balance shifts left/right. In the Imager panel, Width and Corr. show the current stereo width and phase correlation (negative = phase problems).

Delay

Repeats the sound after a short time (an echo). Use sparingly on a master. Solo Wet lets you hear just the echoes.

Stereo delay with independent left/right times, Feedback, Ping-Pong and low/high cuts on the feedback path. Use sparingly in mastering — a short, filtered slap can add depth to sparse material.

Reverb

Adds room or hall ambience. Solo Wet plays only the reverb so you can judge it, then switch it off again.

A compact algorithmic (comb + all-pass) reverb: Room Size, Damping, Width, Pre-Delay, Low Cut and Mix. Solo Wet removes the dry signal so you can hear only the reverb (the Delay has the same switch for its echoes) - switch it off again before bouncing. In mastering, 3–8 % of a small room can "glue" dry stems.

Stem EQ

Turns the vocals, drums, bass or other parts of a finished song up or down a little without having the original stems. It is an estimate, not magic.

Estimates four stems — Bass, Drums, Vocals, Other — and lets you change the level (±12 dB) and tone (±6 dB tilt) of each without opening the mix. Stems are estimated from harmonic-vs-percussive structure and stereo-centre energy, so separation is approximate. Focus sets how hard the masks are (higher = more separation, more artefacts). Solo lets you audition what the algorithm believes each stem to be. For best results keep moves under ±4 dB.

Pitch Correction

Fixes out-of-tune notes. Choose Vocal for singing, Instrument for one melody line, Full Song for a whole mix. Lower retune speed = more natural; higher = robotic effect.

Natural-sounding automatic pitch correction in three modes. It is off by default and sits first in the chain.

ModeUse it onHow it works
VocalA solo vocal, acapella or bounced lead-vocal stemA YIN tracker follows the fundamental frequency. A slowly-moving centre pitch is derived from it, so natural vibrato and expression are left on top of the correction. The nearest note of your key/scale is chosen with hysteresis (no flickering between notes), and the pitch is moved with formant-preserving time-domain PSOLA, so the voice does not turn into a chipmunk or a robot.
InstrumentA monophonic line: guitar lead, bass, synth, brass, stringsSame engine with a wider tracking range and slower defaults; suitable for intonation fixes and bass.

Full Song (tuning)A stereo mixMeasures the overall tuning of the programme against the 12-tone grid (via phase-accurate spectral peaks) and, if you wish, moves the whole spectrum by a few cents with a phase vocoder (shared FFT pass). Vocal / Instrument controls

  • Key / Scale – Chromatic (any semitone), Major, Natural/Harmonic Minor, Major/Minor Pentatonic, Blues, Dorian, Mixolydian. 🎼 Detect key from audio reads a pitch-class histogram collected while the audio plays (Krumhansl-Schmuckler) and sets Key and Scale to Major/Minor for you. Use Clear key memory before analysing a different song.
  • Retune Speed – how long it takes to move to the target note. 0 ms = hard, robotic "effect" tuning; 40 ms = tight modern pop; 90–150 ms = natural; 200+ ms = very gentle.
  • Correction Amount – scales the correction (50 % moves notes half-way to the target).
  • Preserve Vibrato – at 100 % only the slow pitch centre is corrected and all vibrato and slides are kept; at 0 % the pitch is flattened onto the target.
  • Tolerance – deviations smaller than this (in cents) are left untouched, which keeps a natural, slightly imperfect feel.
  • Max Correction – the largest shift ever applied. Keep ≤ 200 cents for natural results.
  • Lowest / Highest Pitch and Voicing Confidence – the range in which pitch is trusted. Breaths, consonants and noise are never retuned; the correction fades out within 20 ms when no pitch is found.
  • A4 Reference – the tuning of your song (415–466 Hz).

The big readout shows the detected note and cents, the target note, and the applied shift; the needle shows detected (red) versus corrected (green) pitch around the target note.

Full Song controls

  • Auto Detect Tuning – corrects slowly (about 10 cents per second) toward Target A4, only when the detection confidence is high; Tuning Amount scales it. Manual Tuning applies a fixed offset instead (e.g. +31.8 cents converts 432 Hz to 440 Hz).
Limits. Monophonic modes expect one pitch at a time; running them on a full mix or on chords will not work. Very large corrections (more than about 3 semitones) always sound processed with any pitch shifter. Latency is about 70 ms in Vocal/Instrument mode (reported to the host).

Codec Preview

Previews how your master might sound after MP3/streaming compression, so you can hear what gets lost. Remember to turn it off before exporting!

A monitoring tool that lets you hear roughly how your master will behave after lossy encoding. It simulates MP3-like, AAC-like and Opus-like encodes at 32-320 kbps: the typical encoder low-pass (for example about 16 kHz at 128 kbps), joint-stereo simplification (the side signal is quantised more coarsely at low bitrates), per-band quantisation that creates spectral “holes” and quantisation noise, and the pre-echo smearing of a long transform window. Listen to Difference plays only what the simulated encode removes - if that sounds musical, the encode will be audible.

It is not a real encoder. The simulation shows tendencies (what is lost first, which master is more fragile), not an exact result. Always switch it off before rendering or exporting your master. It shares the 2048-sample FFT latency with the other FFT modules.

Vintage Limiter

A classic-style limiter that stops peaks from going too loud, with a hardware character.

A fast, coloured peak limiter with selectable Input drive, Attack, Release and Character (saturation). Use it as a tone-shaping stage before the Maximizer; it does not guarantee the ceiling.

Maximizer

The loudness maker. Push the Gain up to get louder; the Ceiling is a wall the sound cannot cross, so it never clips. If it starts to sound squashed or distorted, back off.

The final lookahead limiter. Gain pushes the signal into the Ceiling; the ceiling is a true-peak ceiling when True Peak is on (inter-sample peaks are estimated with 4× interpolation). Release sets the recovery time, Character blends in soft saturation before the limiter, and Stereo Independence lets channels limit separately (0 % = fully linked, best image).

A final 0.8 ms safety stage is always active so the ceiling holds in every mode. The GR meter shows the current gain reduction. As a rule of thumb, more than 6–8 dB of reduction on a full mix will start to sound dense; the Master Assistant warns you above 8 dB.

Dither

Dither is a tiny bit of noise added when exporting to 16-bit so the quiet endings sound smooth. Turn it on only for the very last export, once.

Choose 16 bit, 20 bit or 24 bit in the top bar to add noise-shaped TPDF dither to the final output — only when you render to that bit depth, and only once, at the very end of the chain. Leave it Off when rendering 32-bit float.

7. Master Assistant

The Assistant listens to your song, then suggests settings. Press the button, play the loudest part for at least 5 seconds, and read the suggestions. Apply what you agree with.

Open it with 🎛 Master Assistant.

  1. Choose a Genre (it selects the target tonal balance, limiter algorithm, compression style and minimum stereo width) and a Target: Streaming −14 LUFS / −1 dBTP, Loud −9 LUFS / −0.3 dBTP, Broadcast −23 LUFS / −1 dBTP, or Custom.
  2. Under Modules the assistant may adjust tick which modules it is allowed to touch — your own flow. Modules you leave unticked are never changed.
  3. Click Start listening and play the loudest, most representative section (a chorus). After at least five seconds click Analyze now; 20–30 seconds gives better results.
  4. Read the Measurements (loudness, LRA, true peak, crest factor, width, correlation, sub-versus-bass balance) and the Suggestions. Every suggestion has a plain-language reason.
  5. Click ✔ Apply suggestions. The assistant sets only the parameters it mentions; you can then edit anything. ↶ Undo restores the state from before you applied.

The assistant measures the signal arriving at the plug-in input, so it analyses your unprocessed mix. It suggests: corrective EQ bands (up to five, ±4 dB maximum), a 24 Hz high-pass, gentle dynamics when the crest factor is high, Unlimiter when the mix looks over-limited, sub mono-folding and sub trims, widening or narrowing of the highs, top-end excitement for dull mixes, the Stabilizer for large tonal deviations, and finally Maximizer gain, ceiling and algorithm to reach the loudness target.

How it decides. Tonal advice only uses the reliable 90 Hz - 12 kHz range and compares the shape of your mix to the genre target after removing the overall level. A global brightness difference becomes one broad high shelf at 3 kHz; only prominent narrow excesses get a bell cut (never a narrow boost). Dynamics decisions use the peak-to-loudness ratio (PLR = true peak minus integrated loudness), the compressor threshold follows your measured loudness, and the sub/bass comparison is made per octave. If fewer than five seconds of signal were heard, it suggests nothing.
It guides, you decide. Always check the result by ear, at matched loudness, against a reference track.

Reference A/B player

Lets you compare your master with a professional song at the same loudness, so your ears are not fooled by volume. Louder always sounds "better" at first.

Open Reference A/B player (under the spectrum), type the path of a WAV file (uncompressed PCM or float, up to 6 minutes) and press Load. Then:

  • Master / Reference switches the output between your processed mix and the reference with a 5 ms cross-fade. The meters, spectrum and vectorscope show whatever you are listening to, so you can compare loudness and tonal balance directly.
  • Play / Pause / Restart and the position slider control the reference transport (independent of the host transport).
  • Match loudness to master scales the reference to the integrated loudness of your processed signal (learned while you listen to the master), so you judge tone and dynamics instead of volume. Trim adds a manual offset.

The reference is played back instead of the master only while Reference is selected; it never changes the processing. Remember to switch back to Master before you render.

AirZone 26 Tonal Balance (companion plug-in)

Tonal Balance tells you if your song has too much or too little bass, mids or treble compared with a typical song in the same genre. It only shows; it never changes your sound. Put it last in the chain, play the loudest part, and look at the five gauges: when they are in the grey zone, the balance is normal.

A second plug-in in the package, intended for the end of the master chain (after the limiter). It passes audio through unchanged and shows how the tonal balance of your music compares with a target:

  • Five region gauges (Low 40-200 Hz, Low-Mid 200-800 Hz, Mid 0.8-3 kHz, High-Mid 3-8 kHz, High 8-16 kHz) show the deviation from the target in dB; the grey band is the tolerance (default ±3 dB). Green = inside, amber = outside, red = far outside.
  • A curve view overlays your mix (teal) on the target (amber with a shaded tolerance range).
  • Targets: eight genre targets, or a custom target that you either capture from audio you are currently playing (for example a reference track) or load from a WAV file.
  • Written advice names the regions that are too high or too low and suggests cutting or lifting them.
  • Analysis Memory sets how many seconds of music are averaged. Play the loudest part of the song for 10-20 seconds for a stable picture.

Only the shape of the spectrum is compared (the overall level is removed), so the meter is independent of loudness.

Step-by-step: using Tonal Balance

  1. Insert it last on the master track, after AirZone 26 (and after the limiter). It is a separate plug-in called AirZone 26 Tonal Balance; it does not change the sound.
  2. Pick a target: choose a genre (Pop, Rock, Hip-Hop, EDM, ...) or switch to Custom.
  3. Play the loudest section for 10-20 seconds. The gauges fill in once enough audio has been heard. If you jump around in the song, press Reset to start a fresh average.
  4. Read the gauges. The middle line is "exactly like the target". Bar to the right = too much in that region; to the left = too little. Green inside the grey tolerance zone, amber slightly outside, red far outside.
  5. Read the curve. Teal is your song, amber the target and its shaded range. Where teal leaves the shaded area, that is where the balance is off.
  6. Fix it in AirZone 26, not in the meter: e.g. Low is red-high, so lower the Equalizer's low band or the Bass Control; High is red-low, so add a little high shelf or Clarity. Re-check after each change.
  7. Use your own reference: play a song you love, press Capture current balance (or Load WAV) to make it the custom target, then play your master and match it.

8. Presets

A preset is a saved set of knob positions. Pick one as a starting point, then adjust. Save your own with Save.

Pick presets from the menu in the top bar or step through them with ◀ ▶. A preset is a complete snapshot: applying it first returns every parameter to its default (the Bypass switch and your chain order are left alone) and then sets the preset's values. Preset changes are sent through the host, so they are undoable in REAPER and recordable as automation. Hover a preset name for its description.

Module presets

Every one of the 23 modules also has its own preset menu at the top of its panel (about 145 module presets in total: reverb rooms, delay timings, EQ curves, compressor styles, limiter algorithms, pitch settings and more). A module preset changes only that module: it resets the module's parameters, applies the preset and switches the module on. Use the arrows to step through the list, Reset to return the module to its defaults, and the name box with Save to store your own module presets (Presets/Modules/<module>/ in the user folder below).

A/B compare, tonal target, vectorscope, matched bypass

  • A/B (top bar): two complete settings slots. Click the other letter to store the current state in the active slot and recall the other one; Copy copies the current state into the inactive slot.
  • Show tonal target (under the spectrum): overlays the tonal balance typical for the chosen genre (amber line), aligned to your output between 100 Hz and 8 kHz.
  • Vectorscope (Imager panel): mid/side scope of the output. A thin vertical line is mono, a wide cloud is wide, a horizontal line is out of phase.
  • Loudness-matched bypass (top bar, on by default): when you bypass, the original is level-matched to the processed signal using the momentary loudness difference, so you compare tone and dynamics instead of volume.

10. Mastering workflows

Recipes: follow one step by step if you do not know where to begin.

A. Clean loudness for streaming

  1. Load Streaming −14 LUFS Safe.
  2. Play the loudest chorus; raise the Maximizer Gain until Integrated reads about −14 LUFS.
  3. Check True pk stays below −1 dBTP.

B. Competitive loud master

  1. Start from a genre preset (e.g. Pop Loud or EDM Club Loud).
  2. Fix tone first (EQ, Dynamic EQ), then control dynamics, then raise Gain.
  3. If the mix is already crushed, enable the Unlimiter before pushing further.

C. Matching a reference

  1. Learn Source on your mix, Reference on the reference track.
  2. Enable Match EQ at 50–80 % Amount; enable Stabilizer with "Use Reference" for slow tonal drift correction.
  3. Compare at matched loudness.

D. Fixing a lopsided mix without stems

  1. Open Stem EQ, solo each stem to hear what it grabs.
  2. Make small moves (±2–3 dB). Use the Tone control to brighten or darken a single stem.

E. Tuning a vocal

  1. Put a bounced vocal (or acapella) on its own track and add AirZone 26 with the preset Vocal - Natural Tune.
  2. Press Detect key from audio after playing a verse, or set the key yourself. Choose a scale (Major/Minor, or Chromatic if unsure).
  3. Adjust Retune Speed and Tolerance until the correction is inaudible but the pitch sits right; check the readout.

F. Fixing the tuning of a whole master

  1. Load Full Song - Auto Tune to 440 and play the song for about ten seconds.
  2. If the Detected bar shows a stable offset with good confidence, the correction glides in; otherwise leave it off.

G. Mono and vinyl safety

  1. Bass Control: Sub Mono 100 %, Sub/Bass split 100–150 Hz.
  2. Imager: reduce low-band width; watch Corr. stay positive.

10a. Tutorials: metal, rock and atmospheric pop

Mastering is the last 10 %. 90 % of a great master is a good mix. This tutorial has two parts: Part 1 prepares the individual tracks and buses (you can load AirZone 26 on a track or bus, and use it with only the modules you need), Part 2 is the actual master on the master track. Always compare at equal loudness with a reference song in the same style (use the Reference A/B player).

Part 1: Preparing the mix (before mastering)

AirZone 26 is built as a master chain, but it is a normal plug-in, so you can place it on a track or bus and enable just one or two modules. Use your DAW's own EQ/compressor for the basics; use AirZone where it is special (Pitch Correction, Dynamic EQ, Imager, Exciter). Leave 6 dB of headroom on the mix bus (peaks around -6 dBFS) and keep any limiter off the mix bus: AirZone's Maximizer does that on the master.

Rule of thumb: if you have to boost more than 3 dB in mastering, go back and fix it in the mix. Mastering polishes; it does not repair.

Part 2a: Mastering metal

Metal is dense: distorted guitars, fast double-kick and loud vocals fill every frequency. The goal is power without harshness or mush. The dangers are sizzle (4-8 kHz), boom (100-250 Hz) and clicking kicks that vanish when the limiter works.
  1. Preset start: load a Metal/Loud preset from the Factory catalogue, or begin from a Universal Master and switch off what you do not need.
  2. Master Assistant: press it, play the loudest chorus for 10+ seconds, apply the suggestions. Metal is already loud, so expect it to propose cuts rather than boosts.
  3. Equalizer: high-pass at 25-30 Hz; broad dip -1 to -2 dB at 200-300 Hz if muddy; a small notch (-1.5 dB, narrow) around 3-4 kHz if guitars are fizzy. Optionally +1 dB shelf above 10 kHz for air.
  4. Dynamic EQ / Spectral Shaper: tame the harsh 4-8 kHz only when it peaks (threshold so that it reduces about 1-2 dB on loud hits). This lets you keep brightness in quiet parts.
  5. Bass Control + Low End Focus: mono below 100-120 Hz, tighten the 40-90 Hz region so double-kick stays defined.
  6. Dynamics (multiband): ratio 1.5:1-2:1, slow attack (30+ ms) so the transients pass, ~2 dB reduction on the low band, ~1 dB elsewhere. Do not overcompress: metal needs its punch.
  7. Impact: a little transient boost (+1 to +2 dB) brings back the snare/kick click that limiting reduces.
  8. Exciter: optional, low Tape or Warm on the mids for thickness (5-10 %). Skip on already distorted material if it sounds gritty.
  9. Imager: keep the lows mono, leave mids at 100 %, raise highs to 110-120 %. Check the vectorscope: the cloud should be vertical-ish and symmetrical.
  10. Maximizer: use AZ-2 or AZ-3 for a punchy transparent sound, or AZ-6 (clip + limit) for a hard modern metal master. Ceiling -1.0 dBTP. Raise Gain until you hit about 3-6 dB of gain reduction; stop when the kick starts to sound smaller.
  11. Loudness target: about -9 to -8 LUFS integrated is typical for metal; streaming will turn it down anyway, so do not sacrifice dynamics. Check Codec Preview (then turn it off).
  12. Tonal Balance: put it last, genre Rock/Metal-like target, compare, small fixes only.

Part 2b: Mastering rock

Rock should feel alive and natural: guitars with bite, a punchy snare, vocals in front. You can be a little less loud than metal and keep more dynamics.
  1. Start: a Rock preset or Universal Master. Run the Master Assistant on the loudest chorus.
  2. Equalizer: high-pass 25 Hz, +1 dB at 80-100 Hz for the kick/bass, -1 dB at 250 Hz if boxy, +1 dB at 3 kHz for presence and +1 dB shelf above 10 kHz.
  3. Vintage Comp: ratio 2:1, slow attack (20-30 ms), release auto/fast, 1-2 dB reduction for glue. This is the "rock" sound: the track breathes with the drums.
  4. Vintage Tape or Exciter (Tape): 10-20 % for warmth and mild saturation on the whole mix.
  5. Impact: keep neutral or +1 dB attack on the mid band for a snappy snare.
  6. Imager: mono under 120 Hz; widen the highs slightly (105-115 %). Guitars panned hard L/R do most of the width already.
  7. Maximizer: AZ-1 or AZ-2, ceiling -1.0 dBTP, 2-4 dB of gain reduction. Target roughly -10 to -9 LUFS integrated.
  8. Check: Bypass (loudness matched) and the Reference A/B player with a favourite record. If the master feels flatter than the reference, reduce limiting before adding more of anything.

Part 2c: Mastering atmospheric pop

Atmospheric pop (dream pop, ambient pop, cinematic pop) lives on space, softness and a clear vocal. It should be wide and smooth, not loud and aggressive. Protect the delicate reverb tails and the dynamics.
  1. Start: a Pop / Airy / Gentle preset, or Universal Master with the Maximizer turned down.
  2. Equalizer: high-pass at 20-25 Hz, gentle -1 dB at 300 Hz for clarity, +1 to +2 dB shelf above 8-10 kHz for air. Use Match EQ against a reference you love if you want a quick starting point.
  3. Clarity: a little detail lift (10-25 %) to keep pads and reverb from turning to haze.
  4. Dynamics: very gentle (1.3:1-2:1), slow attack, 1 dB reduction. Preserve the dynamics.
  5. Vintage EQ (Pultec style): low boost with a small cut at the same frequency for round, deep low end; high boost at 10 kHz for silk.
  6. Exciter (Warm) or Vintage Tape: subtle, 5-15 %, for glue and analog warmth.
  7. Imager: widen the mids and highs (110-130 %); keep the bass mono. Verify mono compatibility with the correlation meter (stay above 0). A wide reverb that collapses in mono will vanish on phone speakers.
  8. Reverb (only if needed): a short hall at 5-10 % mix, to glue the elements. Use Solo Wet to hear what you are adding, then turn it off.
  9. Maximizer: AZ-4 or AZ-7 (smooth), ceiling -1.0 dBTP, only 1-3 dB reduction. Target -14 to -11 LUFS integrated; this style sounds better with room to breathe.
  10. Vocal-forward? If the vocal is slightly buried, try Stem EQ and raise Vocals by +1 dB (an estimate, so check for artefacts), or go back to the mix.
These are starting points, not laws. The numbers depend on the mix. Change one thing at a time, compare at equal loudness, and trust your ears (and a few different speakers) over any meter. Finally: switch Codec Preview off before exporting.

Final checklist before export

  • True peak at or below -1 dBTP; Codec Preview and Solo Wet switched off; Bypass off.
  • Integrated loudness in your target range; mono-compatibility checked.
  • Dither on only for the last conversion to 16-bit.
  • Listen once on headphones, speakers and a phone.
